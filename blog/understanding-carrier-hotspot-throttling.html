Understanding Carrier Hotspot Throttling

Published September 16, 2026

If you've ever noticed your phone's hotspot slow to a crawl, or your carrier charge you separately for it, even though you're on an "unlimited" plan, you're not imagining things. Hotspot (tethering) traffic is treated differently from on-device data by most major carriers, and it's worth understanding exactly how and why.

It's not the same bucket as your regular data

Most "unlimited" mobile plans are unlimited for traffic generated directly on the phone, browsing, video, apps. The moment that same connection is shared with a laptop, tablet, or another phone via hotspot, many carriers meter it separately, often with a much smaller cap (commonly 5–15GB) before speeds are throttled down to 2G-like levels, regardless of how much "unlimited" data remains on the base plan.

How carriers actually detect tethering

Carriers generally don't ask permission or require you to "declare" tethering, they infer it from how traffic looks on the wire. Two common signals:

  • TTL (Time To Live) decrementing: every hop a packet takes through a router decreases its TTL by one. A packet that's been routed through your phone's hotspot to a second device typically arrives at the carrier's network with a TTL one lower than a packet generated on the phone itself, a simple, reliable tethering signal.
  • Traffic pattern / deep packet inspection (DPI): multiple simultaneous TCP streams, desktop-only user-agent strings, or traffic volume/timing patterns inconsistent with typical phone usage can also flag a connection as shared.

Neither of these requires the carrier to inspect the content of your traffic, just its shape.

Why carriers do this

Hotspot usage tends to replace a home broadband or secondary-device connection entirely, which is a materially different (and more expensive to serve) usage pattern than typical on-device browsing, so carriers price and cap it separately rather than absorbing it into the same "unlimited" bucket. Whether or not that's the right trade-off for a given customer, it's the economic logic behind it.

What are your actual options?

  1. Pay the carrier's add-on. The straightforward, fully-supported route, if your carrier sells one and the price works for you.
  2. Reduce how "tether-like" your traffic looks. This is the technical category Tether Link Utility falls into: instead of a phone acting as a plain Wi-Fi hotspot (where connected devices' traffic passes through mostly unchanged), the app runs a local SOCKS5 proxy on the phone itself. Connected devices route through that local proxy, which changes the traffic's signature before it ever reaches the carrier's network, see the FAQ for specifics on how this compares across Android, Windows, macOS, and iOS clients.
  3. Use a wired connection where possible. USB tethering has its own detection methods too, so it isn't automatically exempt, but some carriers historically treated it differently than Wi-Fi hotspot.

To be precise about what option 2 actually promises: it changes how tethered traffic looks to the carrier's detection methods, which is why it helps in practice for many users, it isn't a guarantee against every possible detection method any carrier might use now or in the future.
